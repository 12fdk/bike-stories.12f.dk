What size bike a kid actually needs — inseam and standover, not age

Age charts sell bikes; they do not fit them. What decides the size is the child's inseam, the standover gap, and whether they can reach and squeeze the brake levers.

You are in a bike shop, or scrolling a second-hand listing, and the box says "ages 4–8". Your child is five. The box says the bike fits. The box has never met your child.

Age ranges are how manufacturers address the largest possible market with one label — a starting point for shopping, not a verdict on fit. What decides whether a child's bike is right is the child's inseam, the standover gap, and whether they can reach and squeeze the brake levers.

Start with the chart, then stop trusting it

Wheel size is a shortlist, not an answer — though matched against inseam rather than age, it is a useful one:

Wheel size Inseam it usually suits
12 in 35–43 cm (14–17 in)
14 in 41–51 cm (16–20 in)
16 in 46–56 cm (18–22 in)
20 in 56–64 cm (22–25 in)
24 in 61–71 cm (24–28 in)

The ranges overlap, and two 16-inch bikes can differ by several centimetres of standover and reach. Use the table to decide which bikes are worth measuring — then measure them.

How to measure the inseam

Stand the child barefoot and straight against a wall, feet shoulder-width apart. Slide a flat book up between their legs until it rests firmly on the crotch — firmly, the way a saddle will — mark the top edge on the wall, and measure floor to mark. Take it standing, not crouching.

Two different things are measured against that number, and they get confused constantly:

Standover is about the frame. The top tube should sit at least 2–5 cm (1–2 in) below the inseam, so the child can straddle the bike with both feet flat and the frame clear beneath them — the larger gap if they will ride off pavement, where they step off in a hurry on uneven ground. If the top tube meets the crotch, the frame is too big, and nothing adjusts that.

Saddle height is about confidence, and it changes as the child improves:

  • A beginner sits with both feet flat on the ground, which usually puts the saddle around 2.5–4 cm (1–1.5 in) below the inseam. Flat feet are what let a nervous rider stop without thinking.
  • As they get confident, raise it a centimetre at a time, especially if they are struggling through the top of the pedal stroke.
  • Once they are competent, set it so they touch down on the balls of their feet — where the leg extends properly and pedalling stops being hard work.

The brake-reach check

This is the check the charts never cover, and the one that decides whether the child can stop.

Sit them on the bike, hands on the grips, and ask them to brake as if they mean it. They should be able to hook two fingers over each lever — index and middle, the rest still round the grip — and squeeze hard, without twisting the bars towards them, sliding their hand round, or coming off the saddle.

If they cannot, adjust before you conclude anything. Most children's brake levers have a reach-adjust screw at the pivot, a small Phillips or 2–3 mm Allen head; winding it in brings the lever closer to the bar. Then confirm the brake still bites well before the lever reaches the grip. If it now hits the grip, the cable needs taking up — worth having a shop do properly, because brakes are the one system on a child's bike where a bodged adjustment has consequences.

What adjustment cannot fix is handlebar reach. If the child is still stretching with the levers wound all the way in, the distance from saddle to bars is beyond their arms. That is a frame problem, and the next size down is the right bike.

One country-specific note. US federal rules treat a child's bike with a seat height of 635 mm or less as a "sidewalk bicycle": one whose minimum seat height is 560 mm or more must have a footbrake — the coaster brake you get by pedalling backwards — and the reach from bar to lever is capped at 76 mm. A US bike may therefore stop mainly with the pedals. Elsewhere hand brakes are usually the whole braking system, and this check is the difference between a bike the child controls and one they do not.

Why "buy big, they'll grow into it" is a safety problem

It is the most common advice given to parents, and the one to push back on hardest. A child who cannot put a foot down quickly, cannot reach the levers, and has to stretch to the bars is not learning to adapt. The margin between a controlled stop and a fall is measured in centimetres of reach and standover, and an oversized frame removes both at once. The growth-room argument works on adult bikes because an adult compensates with strength and reflexes; a five-year-old who is 2 cm short of the levers does not grow into that by the end of the summer, and every junction until then is a gamble.

Growing room belongs in the seatpost and the stem — the parts designed to be moved. A bike bought to fit, with the saddle raised over a couple of years, is the trade that works.

A worked example: a five-year-old

A child of 110 cm and 18 kg (40 lb), inseam measured at 48 cm.

  • Shortlist. 48 cm sits mid-range for a 16-inch wheel and below the 20-inch range.
  • Standover. The top tube needs to be at or below about 43–46 cm. Most 16-inch bikes clear that; most 20-inch bikes do not.
  • Saddle. Minimum saddle height 44 cm or lower for feet-flat riding — check the spec sheet, since some 16-inch bikes sit too high.
  • Brakes. Two fingers, squeezed hard, no twisting. If it fails, wind the reach screws in and try again before walking away.
  • Weight. At 18 kg of child, a 5.5 kg bike is 31% of body weight and a 10 kg one is 56%. Both exist with a 16-inch wheel.

The 16-inch bike is the call — not because a box says "ages 4–8", but because the inseam, the standover and the brake reach agree.

The weight check most parents skip

Bike weight is a real, checkable spec, and the one nobody looks at. The figure usually quoted is that a child's bike should stay under about 30–40% of the child's body weight, lower being better — a ratio no adult would tolerate, since a 75 kg rider on a 10 kg bike is at 13%.

Plenty of bikes fail the strict version of that, and the rule is still worth using, because the market spread is enormous: lightweight 16-inch bikes come in around 5–6 kg (11–13 lb), mainstream ones commonly 8–11 kg (18–24 lb). What the extra weight costs a child is control at low speed — starting, stopping, holding the bike up at a standstill and catching it when it tips. If a listing does not state a weight, ask. "About 10 kg" is an answer; "not that heavy" is not.

Three situations, three different answers

A three-year-old on a balance bike. Inseam and standover still apply, and feet-flat is the whole point. Brakes vary: plenty have none and are stopped with the feet, but many do have a rear hand brake — where one is fitted, run the same reach check on it.

A five- to seven-year-old on a pedal bike. Where the brake-reach check earns its place. They are stopping with their hands, often for the first time, and the levers were set at the factory for nobody in particular.

A nine-year-old on a geared 24-inch bike. Standover is rarely the problem by then; reach often still is, especially on a small-framed child, and levers on a geared bike sit further out because they carry the shifter too.

When they have outgrown it

The signs arrive in order: the saddle is at its maximum and the leg still bends too much, the knees come up towards the bars, and the standover gap has gone. The bike is then not "nearly still fine" — it is the buying-too-big problem, arriving from the other direction.

Write down the inseam and the date each time you check, and the next decision is easy, including which child the bike moves to next. A notebook does this perfectly well, as does an app like Bike Stories, which keeps fit notes next to each bike's service history. What matters is that the number lives somewhere other than memory — a bike handed down twice usually arrives with a chain nobody has thought about for either of those cycles, and when to replace a bike chain is the same question whatever size the wheels are.

The short version

  1. Measure the inseam: child standing, book at the crotch, floor to book.
  2. Shortlist a wheel size from the inseam, not the age.
  3. Standover: top tube 2–5 cm below the inseam, more for off-road.
  4. Saddle: feet flat for a beginner, balls of the feet once competent — and check the bike's minimum saddle height.
  5. Brakes: two fingers, a hard squeeze, no twisting. Wind the reach screws in before giving up on the bike.
  6. Weight: under about a third of the child's body weight if you can, and take the lighter bike in any given size.
  7. Growth room lives in the seatpost and the stem, never the frame size.

A bike that fits now and gets handed on is the right trade. A bike that is almost right because the box said so is not.

Common questions

How do I know what size bike my child needs?

Measure the child's inseam rather than reading the age on the box: stand them barefoot against a wall, slide a flat book up to the crotch, and measure floor to book. Use that to shortlist a wheel size — roughly 46-56 cm (18-22 in) of inseam for a 16-inch bike, 56-64 cm (22-25 in) for a 20-inch — then check the bike itself. The top tube should sit at least 2-5 cm (1-2 in) below the inseam so the child can straddle it with both feet flat, and the child should be able to hook two fingers over each brake lever and squeeze hard without twisting the bars. A bike that fails the brake check is too big for them even if the wheel size looks right, because a child who cannot reach the brakes cannot stop the bike.

Should I buy a bike bigger so they grow into it?

No — on a child's bike this is a safety decision rather than a matter of taste. A frame that is too big means the child cannot get a foot down quickly, has to stretch to the bars, and often cannot reach the brake levers, so every emergency stop is slower and more likely to end badly. Growing room belongs in the seatpost and the stem, which are designed to be adjusted, not in a frame two sizes up. Buy the bike that fits now, raise the saddle as they grow, and accept that a well-chosen bike will be handed on rather than kept.

My child cannot reach the brake levers. Is the bike too big?

Not necessarily — check the adjustment first. Most children's brake levers have a reach-adjust screw at the pivot, usually a small Phillips or 2-3 mm Allen head, and winding it in brings the lever closer to the bar. Adjust it until the child can hook two fingers over the lever comfortably, then squeeze to confirm the brake still bites well before the lever touches the grip; if it now hits the grip, the cable needs taking up. If the child still has to twist the bars or shift their grip with the lever wound all the way in, the handlebar reach is beyond them and the frame really is too big.

Does bike weight matter for a kid?

Yes, and it is the spec most often ignored. The guidance usually quoted is that a child's bike should stay under about 30-40% of the child's body weight, and lower is better — a heavy bike is hard to hold up at a standstill, hard to catch when it tips, and hard to carry up a kerb or a flight of stairs. The market makes this a real choice: lightweight 16-inch bikes come in around 5-6 kg (11-13 lb) while mainstream ones are commonly 8-11 kg (18-24 lb), so two bikes of the same wheel size can differ by half the child's own weight. If a listing does not state the weight, ask, and be suspicious when the answer is vague.
