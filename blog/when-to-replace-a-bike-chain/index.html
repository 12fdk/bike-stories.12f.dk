When to replace a bike chain (and what it costs if you don't)

A chain is the cheapest part on your bike and the one that costs the most when you ignore it. Here is how to know when yours is done.

A chain is the cheapest moving part on a bicycle and the most expensive one to neglect. Left too long, it stops being a $40 consumable and becomes a $250 drivetrain replacement — and the annoying part is that the failure is entirely predictable, measurable with a $12 tool, and easy to plan around if you know what distance you have ridden on it.

That last condition is the one most people fail. Not because measuring is hard, but because "when did I fit this chain?" is a question almost nobody can answer in kilometres.

What "chain stretch" actually is

The phrase is misleading. The steel side plates are not stretching. What wears is the interface between each pin and the inside of each roller — metal-on-metal, with grit acting as a lapping compound. As material is worn away, every link gains a fraction of a millimetre of play, and across a hundred links that adds up to a chain that is measurably longer than the one you fitted.

This matters because the chain has to mesh with sprocket teeth that were cut for a specific pitch — half an inch per link. A chain whose effective pitch has grown by half a percent no longer sits correctly in the tooth valleys. It rides higher, concentrates load on fewer teeth, and starts machining the sprockets into a matching worn profile. That is the actual damage: the chain wears the cassette, and later the chainrings, into a shape that only a worn chain fits.

Replace it before that happens and the cassette can outlive four or five chains. Replace it after and you are buying both.

The two numbers

Measure the wear, not the calendar.

  • 0.5% — the replacement threshold for 11- and 12-speed drivetrains. Narrow chains and narrow sprockets are less tolerant, and the manufacturers say so.
  • 0.75% — the threshold for 8-, 9- and 10-speed systems, which have more material to give.

If you are unsure which applies, use 0.5%. The cost of being early is one chain; the cost of being late is the whole cassette.

Measuring with a chain checker

A drop-in gauge is the fastest method. Put the pin at one end into a roller, let the other end fall: if it drops fully into the chain at the 0.5 mark, you are at or past 0.5% wear. Two cautions worth knowing:

  1. Cheap two-point gauges include roller wear in the reading, so they tend to condemn a chain slightly early. Not a disaster — early is the safe direction.
  2. Measure in a few places along the chain. Wear is rarely perfectly even, and the worst section is the one that decides.

Measuring with a steel ruler

No gauge, no problem. On a chain under mild tension, line the ruler's zero up with the centre of one pin and count twelve full links along:

  • 12 inches exactly — new.
  • 12 1/16 inches — 0.5% worn. Replace an 11- or 12-speed chain now.
  • 12 1/8 inches — 0.75% worn. Replace anything, and check the cassette for skipping under load.

A metric ruler works the same way: 304.8 mm nominal, replace at about 306.3 mm for 0.5%.

How long should a chain actually last?

Wide ranges, because the variable is grit rather than mileage:

| Riding | Realistic chain life | |---|---| | Dry roads, regularly cleaned and lubed | 4,000–6,000 km | | Mixed commuting, all weather | 2,500–4,000 km | | Winter road, salt and spray | 1,500–2,500 km | | Muddy off-road, gravel in the wet | 1,000–2,000 km | | E-bike with mid-drive motor | 1,500–3,000 km |

E-bikes deserve their own line. A mid-drive motor puts several hundred watts through the same chain a rider alone would load with 150, and torque sensors mean that force arrives early in the pedal stroke. Chains on e-bikes wear noticeably faster, and the good ones are built heavier for exactly that reason.

The point of the table is not to give you a number to trust. It is to show that "about a year" is meaningless when a year could be 900 km or 9,000. What you need is your own distance on that specific chain, which is precisely the number a logbook keeps and memory does not — it is the whole reason Bike Stories counts the distance ridden since a part was fitted and shows the remaining life as a bar.

The arithmetic, plainly

Say a chain costs $45 and a cassette $90, with chainrings at $60 more.

  • Replace on time: four chains over 12,000 km = $180, cassette survives. Total $180.
  • Run each chain to destruction: three chains = $135, plus two cassettes = $180, plus a chainring set eventually = $60. Total $375, and a drivetrain that shifted badly for a good part of those kilometres.

Two chains' worth of money, and the on-time option is also the one that shifts crisply and runs quietly the whole time. Chain wear is one of the few areas of bike maintenance where being fastidious is straightforwardly cheaper.

What to do if you have already left it too late

If the chain measures past 0.75% and the bike has been ridden that way for a while, do not just fit a new chain and hope. Fit the new chain, then ride it somewhere safe and put real force through the pedals in the smallest sprocket you regularly use — the one that has taken the most load. If it skips, the cassette has worn to match the old chain and needs replacing too. Chainrings usually survive one such cycle; if the teeth look like breaking waves, with a hooked profile, they do not.

There is a middle path some people use: keep two or three chains and rotate them every few hundred kilometres so they wear together at a similar rate. It works, it spreads the cost, and it is a lot of bookkeeping for a modest gain. Rotating chains without recording which one is on the bike is worse than not rotating them at all.

A short routine that prevents most of this

  1. Wipe the chain after wet or dirty rides. A rag and thirty seconds removes the grit that does the actual damage.
  2. Re-lube on a schedule that matches conditions, not on a schedule that matches your calendar. Dry conditions: a light lube more often. Wet: a heavier one, and wipe the excess so it does not attract grit.
  3. Measure every few hundred kilometres. It takes ten seconds and it is the only real signal.
  4. Write down the date and distance when you fit a new chain. Everything above depends on knowing how far this particular chain has run.

The last one is the step people skip, and it is the one that makes the other three useful. Whether you keep it in an app, a notebook or a text file matters much less than keeping it at all: the number you want, on the day you are standing over the bike wondering, is "how far since I fitted this?" — and that number has to have been recorded months earlier by someone who did not yet know they would need it. The same logic applies to a child's bike: the chain on the 5-year-old's bike is no less wear-prone just because the rider is smaller, and if you have a family fleet to look after, the sizing question that comes first is covered in our guide on what size bike a kid actually needs.

Common questions

How many kilometres does a bike chain last?

Anywhere from 2,000 to 5,000 km for most riders, and the spread is real rather than vague: a clean chain on a dry-road commuter bike, lubed regularly, can pass 5,000 km, while the same chain on a muddy winter cyclocross bike can be finished at 1,500. Grit is the variable that matters most, because chain wear is abrasion inside the pin and bushing, not fatigue. Measure rather than guess, and use your own recorded distance to learn what your conditions do.

What is 0.5% chain wear?

It is the amount by which a section of chain has grown longer than its nominal length, caused by material wearing away inside each pin and bushing. A new chain has a pitch of exactly half an inch per link; at 0.5% wear, twelve links measure 12 1/16 inches instead of 12. For 11- and 12-speed drivetrains, 0.5% is the replacement point. For 8-, 9- and 10-speed, you usually have until 0.75%.

Can I just keep riding a worn chain?

You can, and it will keep working for a while — that is exactly the trap. A worn chain reshapes the cassette sprockets and eventually the chainrings to match its longer pitch. Once that has happened, a new chain skips under load on the old cassette and you have to replace both. The chain is the sacrificial part; letting it wear past its limit transfers the wear to the parts that cost five times as much.

Does a more expensive chain last longer?

Sometimes, but not proportionally, and cleanliness beats price. A mid-range chain that is wiped and re-lubed every few hundred kilometres will outlast a top-end one run dry through winter. The bigger gains come from keeping grit out of the rollers: wipe the chain after wet rides, use a lube suited to your conditions, and do not degrease so aggressively that you strip the factory lubricant from inside the rollers before its time.
