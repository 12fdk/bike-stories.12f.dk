You are standing in a bike shop, or scrolling a second-hand listing, and the box says "ages 4–8." Your child is 5. The box says the bike fits. The box does not know your child.

Age charts are how manufacturers sell to the largest possible market: one label that covers every plausible buyer. They are a starting point for searching, not a verdict on fit. The two numbers that actually decide whether a child's bike is right are the child's **inseam** and **whether they can reach and squeeze the brake levers**. Everything else — wheel size, frame geometry, how many years they will "outgrow" the frame — is secondary.

## How to measure the inseam (and why it is the one number)

The inseam is the distance from the floor to the top of the child's crotch, measured while they stand barefoot. It determines the saddle height that lets them put both feet flat on the ground when the pedals are level — which is the position they will be in every time they stop, wait at a crossing, or the bike wobbles.

To take it: have the child stand up straight against a wall, feet shoulder-width apart. Slide a book or a flat object between their legs, resting on the crotch, and mark the top of the book on the wall with a pencil. Measure from the floor to the mark. That is the inseam. Do this while they are standing, not crouching or leaning — a crouched measurement reads too small.

**The standover check:** the top tube (the horizontal bar the child's legs straddle) should sit at least **2–5 cm (1–2 in) below the inseam**. That gap lets them stand over the bike with both feet flat and the frame between their legs, ready to step down the instant the bike starts to move or tip. If the top tube is at or above the inseam, the bike is too big and they will not be able to stand over it properly.

If you do not have the bike in hand, you can still check: most manufacturers list the standover height on their spec page, and second-hand sellers can measure the top tube height with a tape. Ask before you buy.

## The brake-reach test (the one that actually matters)

This is the check that wheel-size charts never cover, and the one that separates a bike a child can control from one they cannot.

Put the child on the bike with the saddle at the height that lets them put a foot flat on the ground. Ask them to reach for both brake levers, as if they are braking hard. The correct test:

- Can they reach both levers **without twisting the handlebars** to get to them?
- Can they **squeeze both levers to full travel** (or close to it) with the bike under normal load, not just when the levers are already partway in?
- Do they have to **lean forward or stand on the pedals** to reach them?

If the answer to any of these is no, the bike is too big — not because the frame is two sizes up, but because the handlebar reach or the lever pull is beyond the child's arm and hand. A child who cannot reach the brakes cannot stop the bike, and on a hill or at a crossing, that is the entire problem.

**This is not something you can fix with a new set of shorter levers.** Shorter levers do close some of the gap, but on a frame that is genuinely too long for the child's arms, the remaining reach still has to be made up by twisting the bars or standing on the pedals, and the child's hand is too small to wrap around a short lever and squeeze it hard anyway. The honest answer for a child who cannot reach the levers on a given frame is: this frame is too big for them, and the next size down is the right one.

## Why "buy big, they will grow into it" is a safety problem

This is the most common piece of sizing advice given to parents, and it is the one to push back on hardest.

A child who cannot put a foot down quickly, cannot reach the brake levers, and has to stretch to the bars is not "learning to adapt" — they are in a situation where every stop is harder and more likely to go wrong. The margin between a controlled stop and a fall is measured in centimetres of reach and centimetres of standover, and a frame that is too big removes both margins at once.

The "growth room" argument works on adult bikes because an adult can compensate with strength and reflexes. A child cannot. A 5-year-old who is 2 cm short of the brake levers on a "growth-room" bike is not going to develop the extra 2 cm by the end of the summer — the geometry is what it is.

**Growing room belongs in the seatpost and the stem, not in a frame two sizes up.** A bike with a long-travel seatpost and a short stem gives you the ability to adjust the fit as the child grows, without changing the frame size. That is where the room is.

## A worked example: a 5-year-old

A 5-year-old child, 110 cm tall, weighing 18 kg (40 lb). Inseam measured: 42 cm (16.5 in).

- **Standover check:** the top tube should sit at or below about 37–40 cm (14.5–15.7 in), which is 2–5 cm below the 42 cm inseam. A 16-inch wheel bike typically has a top tube in that range; a 20-inch bike usually does not.
- **Brake reach:** on a 16-inch frame with the saddle set to let the foot flat on the ground, check the levers. Most 16-inch kids' bikes are designed for this age range and will pass the reach test. A 20-inch frame will often fail it.
- **Weight check:** one-fifth of the child's 18 kg is 3.6 kg (8 lb), which is the ceiling to aim for. In practice, most 16-inch kids' bikes weigh 8–11 kg (18–24 lb) — well over a fifth, but still manageable for a child of this weight. A 20-inch steel bike at 14 kg (31 lb) is a lot harder for the same child to control or catch when it tips. The lighter bike in the same size is the better choice, and the weight gap between a 16-inch and a 20-inch frame is usually large enough to matter.

The 16-inch bike is the right call here, not because the box says "ages 4–8," but because the inseam, standover, and brake-reach checks all pass.

## The weight check most parents skip

Bike weight is a real, checkable spec, and it is the one most parents never look at. A bike that weighs more than about **one-fifth of the child's own weight** is hard for them to control, lift, or carry. A heavy bike that tips over is harder to catch, which makes falls more likely and more serious.

When comparing two bikes in the same size, the lighter one is almost always the better choice for a child, even at a modest price premium. A 9 kg aluminium kids' bike and a 14 kg steel one in the same wheel size are not equivalent: the lighter one is easier to start, stop, and recover from a tip, and that advantage compounds over every ride.

If a listing does not state the weight, ask the seller. "The bike is about 10 kg" is a useful answer; "it's not that heavy" is not.

## What this means for the three common situations

**A 3-year-old on a balance bike.** Sizing here is different from a pedal bike. The frame should let them straddle with a comfortable gap, and the saddle should be low enough for both feet flat on the ground. Balance bikes have no brakes to reach (they are stopped by the feet), so the brake-reach test does not apply. The inseam and standover checks still do.

**A 5–7-year-old on a pedal bike with hand brakes.** This is where the brake-reach test matters most. The child is using hand levers to stop the bike, and if they cannot reach them, the bike is too big. Inseam and standover checks apply as above.

**A 9-year-old on a geared 24-inch bike.** By this age the child is tall enough that most 24-inch frames will pass the standover check, but the brake-reach test still applies — a small-framed 9-year-old on a 24-inch bike can still fail the reach test. Check all three numbers before assuming the age range means the fit is right.

## The short version

1. Measure the inseam: child standing, book at crotch, floor to book = inseam.
2. Check standover: top tube should be 2–5 cm (1–2 in) below the inseam.
3. Check brake reach: child on the bike, saddle at ride height — can they reach and squeeze both levers without twisting the bars?
4. Check weight: bike should weigh no more than about one-fifth of the child's weight.
5. Buy the size that passes all four, not the size the box says fits their age.
6. For growth room, look at seatpost and stem travel, not frame size.
7. When the chain on the 5-year-old's bike is stretched past its limit, the same logic as on an adult bike applies — a worn chain takes the cassette with it, which is covered in our guide on [when to replace a bike chain](/blog/when-to-replace-a-bike-chain/). If you are keeping a family fleet, a maintenance log — paper, a notebook, or an app like Bike Stories — keeps the distance-since-last-service number for each bike, so you are not guessing when the chain is due. A notebook works perfectly well; the point is that the number is written down somewhere.

A bike that fits now and is replaced when the child outgrows it is the right trade. A bike that is "almost right" because the box said so is not.
