A chain is the cheapest moving part on a bicycle and the most expensive one to
neglect. Left too long, it stops being a $40 consumable and becomes a $250
drivetrain replacement — and the annoying part is that the failure is entirely
predictable, measurable with a $12 tool, and easy to plan around if you know
what distance you have ridden on it.

That last condition is the one most people fail. Not because measuring is hard,
but because "when did I fit this chain?" is a question almost nobody can answer
in kilometres.

## What "chain stretch" actually is

The phrase is misleading. The steel side plates are not stretching. What wears
is the interface between each **pin** and the inside of each **roller** —
metal-on-metal, with grit acting as a lapping compound. As material is worn
away, every link gains a fraction of a millimetre of play, and across a hundred
links that adds up to a chain that is measurably longer than the one you fitted.

This matters because the chain has to mesh with sprocket teeth that were cut for
a specific pitch — half an inch per link. A chain whose effective pitch has grown
by half a percent no longer sits correctly in the tooth valleys. It rides higher,
concentrates load on fewer teeth, and starts machining the sprockets into a
matching worn profile. That is the actual damage: the chain wears the cassette,
and later the chainrings, into a shape that only a worn chain fits.

Replace it before that happens and the cassette can outlive four or five chains.
Replace it after and you are buying both.

## The two numbers

Measure the wear, not the calendar.

- **0.5%** — the replacement threshold for **11- and 12-speed** drivetrains. Narrow
  chains and narrow sprockets are less tolerant, and the manufacturers say so.
- **0.75%** — the threshold for **8-, 9- and 10-speed** systems, which have more
  material to give.

If you are unsure which applies, use 0.5%. The cost of being early is one chain;
the cost of being late is the whole cassette.

### Measuring with a chain checker

A drop-in gauge is the fastest method. Put the pin at one end into a roller, let
the other end fall: if it drops fully into the chain at the 0.5 mark, you are at
or past 0.5% wear. Two cautions worth knowing:

1. Cheap two-point gauges include **roller wear** in the reading, so they tend to
   condemn a chain slightly early. Not a disaster — early is the safe direction.
2. Measure in a few places along the chain. Wear is rarely perfectly even, and
   the worst section is the one that decides.

### Measuring with a steel ruler

No gauge, no problem. On a chain under mild tension, line the ruler's zero up
with the centre of one pin and count twelve full links along:

- **12 inches exactly** — new.
- **12 1/16 inches** — 0.5% worn. Replace an 11- or 12-speed chain now.
- **12 1/8 inches** — 0.75% worn. Replace anything, and check the cassette for
  skipping under load.

A metric ruler works the same way: 304.8 mm nominal, replace at about 306.3 mm
for 0.5%.

## How long should a chain actually last?

Wide ranges, because the variable is grit rather than mileage:

| Riding | Realistic chain life |
|---|---|
| Dry roads, regularly cleaned and lubed | 4,000–6,000 km |
| Mixed commuting, all weather | 2,500–4,000 km |
| Winter road, salt and spray | 1,500–2,500 km |
| Muddy off-road, gravel in the wet | 1,000–2,000 km |
| E-bike with mid-drive motor | 1,500–3,000 km |

E-bikes deserve their own line. A mid-drive motor puts several hundred watts
through the same chain a rider alone would load with 150, and torque sensors mean
that force arrives early in the pedal stroke. Chains on e-bikes wear noticeably
faster, and the good ones are built heavier for exactly that reason.

The point of the table is not to give you a number to trust. It is to show that
"about a year" is meaningless when a year could be 900 km or 9,000. What you
need is your own distance on that specific chain, which is precisely the number a
logbook keeps and memory does not — it is the whole reason **Bike Stories** counts
the distance ridden since a part was fitted and shows the remaining life as a bar.

## The arithmetic, plainly

Say a chain costs $45 and a cassette $90, with chainrings at $60 more.

- **Replace on time:** four chains over 12,000 km = $180, cassette survives. Total
  $180.
- **Run each chain to destruction:** three chains = $135, plus two cassettes = $180,
  plus a chainring set eventually = $60. Total $375, and a drivetrain that shifted
  badly for a good part of those kilometres.

Two chains' worth of money, and the on-time option is also the one that shifts
crisply and runs quietly the whole time. Chain wear is one of the few areas of
bike maintenance where being fastidious is straightforwardly cheaper.

## What to do if you have already left it too late

If the chain measures past 0.75% and the bike has been ridden that way for a
while, do not just fit a new chain and hope. Fit the new chain, then ride it
somewhere safe and put real force through the pedals in the smallest sprocket you
regularly use — the one that has taken the most load. If it skips, the cassette
has worn to match the old chain and needs replacing too. Chainrings usually
survive one such cycle; if the teeth look like breaking waves, with a hooked
profile, they do not.

There is a middle path some people use: keep two or three chains and rotate them
every few hundred kilometres so they wear together at a similar rate. It works,
it spreads the cost, and it is a lot of bookkeeping for a modest gain. Rotating
chains without recording which one is on the bike is worse than not rotating them
at all.

## A short routine that prevents most of this

1. **Wipe the chain after wet or dirty rides.** A rag and thirty seconds removes the
   grit that does the actual damage.
2. **Re-lube on a schedule that matches conditions**, not on a schedule that matches
   your calendar. Dry conditions: a light lube more often. Wet: a heavier one, and
   wipe the excess so it does not attract grit.
3. **Measure every few hundred kilometres.** It takes ten seconds and it is the only
   real signal.
4. **Write down the date and distance when you fit a new chain.** Everything above
   depends on knowing how far this particular chain has run.

The last one is the step people skip, and it is the one that makes the other
three useful. Whether you keep it in an app, a notebook or a text file matters
much less than keeping it at all: the number you want, on the day you are
standing over the bike wondering, is "how far since I fitted this?" — and that
number has to have been recorded months earlier by someone who did not yet know
they would need it.
